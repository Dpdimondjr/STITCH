Motivation

Why a flat-field isn't enough

TESS flux offsets come from two sources. The static detector pattern — fixed pixel sensitivity, column-level bias — is small and could in principle be calibrated once. The sector-to-sector variation — driven by re-pointing, thermal state, scattered light, background — changes every sector and is 3.5× larger.

PDC partially corrects systematic effects but does not model this cross-sector component. STITCH was built specifically to address it.

Finding 1

The systematic is spatially coherent on the detector — and rotates between sectors

Background — what is a TESS FFI? TESS observes the sky with four cameras, each containing four CCDs. Each CCD records a 2048×2048 pixel image of a patch of sky roughly every 10 minutes — these are called Full Frame Images (FFIs). Each pixel covers about 21 arcseconds; stars appear as blobs 2–3 pixels wide. Below is a real 50×50 px region from Cam 3, CCD 3 in sector 29.

The key observation. TESS observes the same region of sky — Cam 4's continuous viewing zone (CVZ), a patch that stays on the same camera for every sector — and we can track 440 individual stars across 35 sectors. Each sector, TESS re-points slightly, so those stars land at different column and row positions on the detector. The cross-sector plot below shows those stars at their fixed sky positions (RA/Dec), coloured by the flux offset measured that sector. The gradient direction and sign change completely between sectors — that is only possible if the offset is being driven by detector position, not by anything happening to the stars themselves.

Locality — neighbouring stars share the same offset. Within a single sector on Cam 3, CCD 3, we measured the Pearson correlation between each star's LOO offset and the mean LOO offset of its 15 nearest neighbours (in detector col/row space, within the same CCD). The median correlation across all star pairs is r = 0.51. In plain terms: knowing what offset your nearest neighbour received predicts about half the variance in your own offset. The systematic is not random star-by-star noise — it varies smoothly across the detector surface, so nearby stars are affected similarly.

Finding 2

STITCH recovers the spatial pattern from detector context alone — no sky coordinates

The model takes only detector inputs: column, row, sector, cam, CCD, and photometric quality features (jitter, PDC noise, crowding). No RA or Dec is ever provided. Running it on the same CVZ patch across sectors 1, 4, 9, and 13: the predicted gradient direction is correct in every sector.

Sector 1 is predicted warm. Sector 4 is predicted blue. Sector 9 is predicted flat. The model has learned the sector-dependent detector response from training data alone — not from any sky information.

Why this matters for locality: The model learned that high-jitter sectors and elevated PDC noise consistently produce biased offsets across a CCD — a cross-sector temporal systematic. This explains why STITCH achieves 38% improvement on quiet stars even though the spatial neighbour correlation ceiling is only ~17%. The extra signal comes from sector-level quality features, not just position.
Experiments

What we tried to improve on LOO labels

To train STITCH, each (star, sector) observation needs a label — a number saying how much that star's flux was offset from its true value in that sector. We tested three ways to define this label.

LOO (leave-one-out). For a given star and sector, take the star's median flux in that sector and divide by its median flux averaged across all other sectors. This measures: "how different is this sector compared to this star's own personal baseline?" It requires the star to be observed in multiple sectors but captures each star's individual history.

LOO label for one star across sectors:
S1: 1.012   S2: 0.998   S3: ?   S4: 1.003   S5: 0.994
LOO for S3 = fluxS3 / mean(S1, S2, S4, S5)  ← star's own history is the reference

Spatial smoothing. Instead of using each star's own cross-sector history, fit a smooth 2D surface (polynomial) to all stars' LOO values on the same CCD in the same sector, and use the fitted value at each star's position as the label. The label now depends only on where on the detector the star sits — not on what sector it came from for that individual star.

Spatial label — one sector, many stars on the same CCD:
★ col=200, row=100: LOO=1.011   ★ col=210, row=95: LOO=1.008
★ col=800, row=500: LOO=0.997   ★ col=1500, row=900: LOO=0.993
Fit smooth surface → label(col, row)  ← neighbours are the reference, not the star's own history

Intra-sector bins. Same idea as spatial smoothing but coarser — divide each CCD into a 4×4 grid and assign each star the median LOO of all stars in its grid cell that sector. Simpler and more robust to outliers.

Result: LOO wins every time.

LOO (pdc_full): 28.7% Intra-sector bins: 25.1% Spatial polynomial: 23.9%

Peer-comparison labels consistently lose ~4 pp of scatter reduction. The reason: each star has genuine per-star variation in its offset — driven by exactly where on its pixel it sits (sub-pixel PRF), how crowded it is, its contamination history. That variation is real signal STITCH can learn if the label preserves it. Spatial averaging discards it. The model already learns the smooth spatial trend from (col, row) inputs — pre-smoothing the label only removes the per-star signal that was still recoverable.

On hurt stars (12% of stars get worse after correction): The single strongest predictor is how many sectors a star was observed in — hurt stars have a median of 5.5 sectors vs 11.0 for improved stars. With only 4–6 sectors, the LOO denominator (the star's own personal baseline) is estimated from very few observations and is noisy. STITCH trains on that noisy label and can apply corrections in the wrong direction at inference. Hurt stars are scattered randomly across the detector — this is a label quality problem, not a spatial one.
